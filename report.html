1 Introduction

The profileration of machine learning systems means that they frequently encounter novel situations. Thus, they need inference time adaptation (ITA) strategies for effective deployment. Two broad ITA strategies exist---test-time-training, or adapter generation---which can be distinguished in how they distribute compute. Test-time training leverages compute at inference time to deal with each situation in turn, whereas adapter generation amortizes compute during a pre-training phase to enable quick situation adaption.

Our focus is on adapter generation strategies, which have proven effective at program generation [PAWs], task adaptation [T2l], and context generation [D2l]. Examples of adapters include Low-Rank-Adapters (LoRAs), prefix tuning, and activation steering. Adapters typically constitute a relatively low-dimensional augmentation of the machine learning system relative to its weights. Full weight generation was an initial focus of the field, although for foundation models the focus has shifted to these adapters; weight generation remains relevant in settings involving simpler and smaller architecutres, such as implicit neural representations.

Research exploring adapter generation have predominantly considered the use of LoRAs. LoRAs were not proposed as adapters for ITA, they were constructed to facilitate efficient adaptation through optimization on small datasets. LoRAs consist of two-rank factors which are multiplied and added linearly to a layer's weight matrix; these operations are invariant to various symmetrics that may actual hinder their generation. This raises the questions: what is the optimal adapter for facilitation inference time adapation? AdapterBench is introduced to answer this question.

Concretely, a codec defines how many numbers the hypernetwork emits and how they turn into a live update — a bilinear B·A product for LoRA/LoKr, an elementwise multiplicative mask for IA3, a dense Fourier-domain reconstruction for FourierFT, an additive vector for activation steering. A hook site says where that update attaches: a named linear submodule (q_proj, v_proj, ...) for weight-space codecs, or the whole decoder layer's residual stream (a sentinel hook named block) for activation-space ones. Everything else — the training loop, the data pipeline, the downstream evaluator — is identical for every adapter that plugs into these two seams.

Two settings

The harness runs two structurally different experiments, covered in detail in §3 and §4:

  • Setting 1 — disk-artifact checkpoint reproduction. Load a released Text-to-LoRA checkpoint, generate a LoRA for a benchmark task's description, materialize it as a real PEFT adapter directory, and score it exactly the way the paper does. No training of our own — a pure reproduction check across every interpreter SakanaAI released a checkpoint for.
  • Setting 2 — live end-to-end SFT. Hook the hypernetwork's generated output directly into a real frozen interpreter's forward pass on real training examples, back-propagate ordinary next-token cross-entropy through the hook into the hypernetwork, and evaluate the same way. No oracle adapters, no reconstruction target — this is the setting where LoRA, IA3, FourierFT, LoKr, FreezeALoRA, and activation steering are actually compared head to head.

Scoring, not scoring-adjacent

Every evaluated model generates a free-text answer against the paper's own prompt templates and 3-shot in-context setup where applicable, then extracts a leading choice letter/digit or a loose true/false keyword — the same generation-plus-extraction protocol Text-to-LoRA's own eval code uses. A log-likelihood-over-choices scorer answers a different question ("which full answer text is most probable") than what published numbers measure, so it was never used here despite being the more common shortcut. Setting 1 scores through vLLM specifically, matching the paper's own inference stack.

3 Setting 1 — reproducing Text-to-LoRA's own checkpoints

Before trusting any comparison the harness produces, it has to first reproduce the results that are already published.

For each of SakanaAI's three released checkpoints, the harness loads the trained hypernetwork, generates one LoRA per benchmark task from that task's pooled description, materializes it as a real PEFT adapter directory, and scores it through vLLM at the paper's own evaluation scale (n=1000/task, n=300 for GSM8K). No gradient updates happen anywhere in this setting — it measures only whether generation and scoring reproduce the published pipeline faithfully.

Result

The generated LoRA outperforms the frozen baseline on 12 of 15 model–task combinations. All three exceptions involve GSM8K or BoolQ, never the other three tasks, and GSM8K is the only task where the frozen baseline wins outright on two of three models (Gemma, Llama) — a consistent pattern across model families rather than a fluke of one checkpoint. Mistral shows the largest LoRA-vs-frozen gap by far, almost entirely driven by its frozen baseline collapsing on Hellaswag (30.8%) — Mistral-7B-Instruct's un-prompted continuations frequently ignore the task's own answer format, a failure the generated LoRA corrects.

The Llama‑3.1‑8B checkpoint reproduces cleanly on the first attempt, across all five tasks — the first time this project has evaluated a third SakanaAI-released interpreter, and the first confirmation that this reproduction pipeline generalizes beyond the two models (Gemma, Mistral) it was originally built against.

4 Setting 2 — live end-to-end SFT, from scratch

Setting 1 only ever generates a LoRA. The real question — what happens with the other five representations — needs a hypernetwork that's never seen a released checkpoint at all.

A hypernetwork is trained entirely from scratch against a frozen, ungated Qwen3‑0.6B interpreter. Its generated output is hooked directly into the interpreter's forward pass on real training examples, and ordinary next-token cross-entropy is back-propagated through the hook into the hypernetwork's own weights — no oracle adapters, no reconstruction target, no released checkpoint anywhere in the loop.

Training recipe

The run matches Text-to-LoRA's own published SFT recipe as closely as this harness's data pipeline allows, so that eventual comparisons against their numbers are not confounded by an unrelated hyperparameter choice:

  • Data: the full 479-task decontaminated Lots-of-LoRAs split T2L itself trains on (hyper_lora_decontam_lol_tasks.yaml's train_ds_names), not a small pilot subset.
  • Schedule: batch size 4, gradient accumulation 64 (effective batch 256), 380 optimizer steps (~10 epochs over the sampled batches), learning rate 1e‑5 with a linear warmup over the first 10% of steps then held constant — all matching T2L's own configuration.
  • Seeds: three seeds per adapter (777 / 778 / 779), each with its own deterministically-seeded data order and hypernetwork initialization, to guard against single-seed noise driving the comparison.
  • Held-out scoring: real BoolQ/Hellaswag examples (n=60/family) never seen during training, scored the same generation-plus-extraction way as Setting 1.

Six representations, one hook each

Every adapter uses a fixed default hook site: LoRA, FreezeALoRA, LoKr, and FourierFT attach to q_proj/v_proj; IA3 attaches to k_proj/v_proj/down_proj; activation steering hooks the residual stream directly (the block sentinel). All six train under the identical loop, data pipeline, and evaluator described in §2 — only the codec and hook site differ.

Result

Five of six representations beat the frozen baseline on Hellaswag consistently across all three seeds, with IA3 the strongest and most stable (35.6% ± 3.5, versus 23.3% frozen) — the same adapter that never collapsed in every earlier single-seed pilot this project ran, now confirmed under a proper multi-seed replication at full training scale. LoKr and activation steering are close behind. FourierFT is a genuine, seed-independent failure, not noise: it scores exactly 0.0% on Hellaswag in all three seeds and swings wildly on BoolQ (27.8% ± 18.4, individual seeds ranging 10–47%) — a real collapse, reproducible at this training scale rather than an artifact of the earlier unseeded pilots. FreezeALoRA is the one representation that slightly underperforms the frozen baseline on BoolQ (71.1% vs. 75.0%), despite beating it clearly on Hellaswag.

This is the full 479-task, 380-step, three-seed run described above — the largest and most controlled comparison this project has run. Unlike the smaller pilots earlier in this project's history, every seed here used its own correctly-seeded data order and hypernetwork initialization throughout, so the spread reported above reflects genuine training variance, not a seeding bug.
